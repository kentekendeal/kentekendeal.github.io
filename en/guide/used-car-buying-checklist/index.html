Buying a used car: 15-point checklist

Go through this list before you pay. It helps you avoid a bad buy, paying too much, or a car with hidden problems.

Before you go and look

  1. Check the licence plate. The RDW (the Dutch vehicle authority) publishes the year, the APK (MOT) expiry date, a judgement on the mileage and any open recalls for every Dutch plate. Do the make, model and colour match the ad? If not, walk away. KentekenDeal shows all of this at once, with an estimate of the value.
  2. Compare the price. Look at similar cars (same model, year and mileage) and enter the asking price on KentekenDeal. Is it much lower than the rest? Then be extra careful.
  3. Is the car imported? An imported car isn't necessarily bad, but its history from before the import is harder to check. Also read what to check on an imported car.
  4. Ask for the service history. A service book or invoices show whether the car was maintained on time. Big jobs (such as the timing belt) are expensive if they're still due.

At the viewing

  1. Mileage. Compare the odometer with the service invoices and APK reports. The RDW data shows whether the mileage is judged logical (Dutch: logisch). Wear (steering wheel, pedals, seat) that doesn't match the mileage is a red flag.
  2. Damage and paint. In daylight, look for colour differences between panels, uneven gaps and overspray on rubber seals. Bring a paint thickness gauge if you can.
  3. Rust and leaks. Look under the car, in the wheel arches and under the bonnet. Oil or coolant stains under the car are a reason to ask questions.
  4. Tyres. The legal minimum is 1.6 mm of tread, but for safety you want clearly more. Unevenly worn tyres can point to an alignment problem.
  5. Interior and electronics. Test the air conditioning, windows, lights, infotainment, camera and sensors. Watch for warning lights on the dashboard after starting.
  6. Test drive. Ideally start the engine cold. Pay attention to shifting, the clutch, the brakes, driving straight and strange noises. Also drive a stretch on the motorway.
  7. Have it inspected. In doubt? A pre-purchase inspection (aankoopkeuring) at an independent garage or the ANWB costs money, but far less than a bad buy.

Buying and paying

  1. Check the paperwork. To transfer the car, the seller needs the registration card (kentekencard) and the transfer code (tenaamstellingscode). Does the seller's name match the owner?
  2. Is the car free of finance or lease? A car that is still on lease or finance often can't simply be sold. Ask about it and ask for proof of ownership.
  3. Dealer or private seller? A dealer gives you more protection and often a warranty. From a private seller you usually buy "as is". In that case, write a short sales agreement with what you agreed and the defects you saw.
  4. Pay safely and arrange insurance. Don't pay in advance without seeing the car and the papers. Have the car transferred to your name (at an RDW desk, a post office with RDW service or an authorised company) and make sure your insurance starts the moment the car is in your name. In the Netherlands the car must be insured from that moment.
